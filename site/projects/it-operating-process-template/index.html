Getting started

Overview & how to use this template

This document defines how your IT organization operates its six core processes. It is designed to be adopted incrementally: start with Incident and Service Request, add Change, mature into Problem and Knowledge, and run Asset Management as the data backbone underneath all of them. Each process chapter is self-contained but cross-references the others, because no IT process succeeds in isolation.

How to customize

1. Click Edit template in the top-right corner.
2. Replace organization name, owners, roles, and any step that doesn't match how you work. Every table cell is editable.
3. Delete what you don't need — fewer, honest process steps beat aspirational ones.
4. Click Save my copy to download your customized version. Re-open it any time to keep editing.
5. Use Print / PDF for a formatted document to circulate for sign-off.

Adoption sequence (recommended)

Weeks 1–4: Incident + Service Request live in InvGate Service Management with the service catalog and priority matrix configured.
Weeks 5–8: Change Enablement with the no-code Workflow Builder and approvals.
Weeks 9–12: Knowledge base seeded from resolved tickets; Problem Management begins with your top 5 recurring incidents.
Weeks 13–16: IT Asset Management: InvGate Asset Management discovery deployed, inventory reconciled, lifecycle and disposal procedures live.

How this template maps to InvGateEvery process step in this document names the InvGate Service Management capability that implements it — service catalog, no-code Workflow Builder, approvals, priority matrix, SLAs/OLAs, AI Hub, knowledge base, dashboards, and the ticket audit trail. Where a step touches InvGate Asset Management, it is tagged in purple. Configuration notes assume administrator access.
A note on right-sizingThis template is sized for mid-market IT teams (roughly 5–50 IT staff). One person can hold several roles — what matters is that each accountability in the RACI is assigned to somebody. Do not create a CAB of ten people if three will do. KPI targets and P1–P4 SLA times are sensible defaults; adjust them to your organization.

Process 1

Incident Management

Click any step in the diagram to open its instructions below · purple pills jump to connected processes · the diagram stays pinned while you scroll.

Restore normal service operation as quickly as possible when something breaks, and minimize the impact on the business. Speed first, root cause later — root cause belongs to Problem Management.

Goals

• Restore service within agreed SLA targets, prioritized by business impact.
• Give users a single, effortless way to report issues and see status.
• Capture clean incident data (category, CI, resolution) so Problem Management has something to mine.
• Contain major incidents with a rehearsed, named-role response.

Procedures

1.1 — Logging & categorization (L1 Agent)
  1. If the ticket arrived via portal, AI agent, or email, verify the auto-captured details; for calls/walk-ups, create the ticket in InvGate Service Management before troubleshooting — no ticket, no work.
  2. Confirm the reporter, location, and affected service. Select the category from the tree (max 3 levels deep — if agents can't find a category in 10 seconds, the tree is too deep).
  3. Write a symptom-based summary: what the user cannot do, since when, error text verbatim. Avoid diagnosis in the summary.
  4. Check for duplicates and existing outages; if a matching major incident exists, link this ticket as a child so users are informed together.
  5. If it's actually a "how do I / I need" — convert to a Service Request (type conversion preserves history).
1.2 — Prioritization & the priority matrix (L1 Agent)
  1. Set Impact: High = whole org or critical business service; Medium = department/team; Low = single user.
  2. Set Urgency: High = work stopped, no workaround; Medium = degraded, workaround exists; Low = inconvenience.
  3. InvGate Service Management's priority matrix computes priority: P1 (High/High) target 4h resolve · P2 8h · P3 2 business days · P4 5 business days. Adjust targets to your SLAs and encode them in InvGate Service Management so the timers match this document.
  4. Never lower a priority to protect an SLA. If priority changes, note why — the audit trail records who and when automatically.
1.3 — Escalation (L1 → L2/L3)
  1. Escalate when: you've exhausted KB and standard diagnostics, ~15 minutes on P1/P2, or the fix requires privileges/skills you don't have.
  2. Reassign to the correct InvGate Service Management group (not a named individual) with the escalation package: symptoms, steps tried, diagnostics, user impact, business context.
  3. Use @-mentions to consult specialists without transferring ownership — the assignee remains responsible.
  4. SLA-risk automation: InvGate Service Management workflow notifies the Incident Manager at 75% of SLA elapsed, and the Service Desk Manager on breach.
  5. Never bounce a ticket back without a comment explaining what's missing. Two bounces = Incident Manager arbitrates.
1.4 — Major incident procedure (Incident Manager)
  1. Declare: any P1 affecting a critical service or ≥30% of users. When in doubt, declare — you can always stand down. Mark the ticket with the Major Incident flag (custom field) which triggers the MI workflow.
  2. Roles: Incident Manager commands; a scribe keeps the timeline in the ticket; a communications lead owns stakeholder updates. Command ≠ fixing: the commander coordinates, specialists fix.
  3. Communicate: post a portal banner/announcement immediately; stakeholder updates every 30 minutes for P1 (even if the update is "no update"). Users find status themselves instead of calling.
  4. Fix: use the emergency change procedure (see Change Enablement) for any production fix — approval recorded, even if verbal first and documented within 24h.
  5. Stand down: confirm restoration with affected users, close comms, and schedule the post-incident review within 5 business days. A problem record is mandatory for every major incident.
1.5 — Resolution, verification & closure (L1 Agent)
  1. Record the resolution code and a note a future agent could reuse: cause of occurrence, fix applied, verification done.
  2. Attach the KB article used. If none existed, create a draft from the ticket (see Knowledge procedure 5.1) — this is how the KB grows from real demand.
  3. Move to Resolved; the user gets a portal/email prompt to confirm. Confirmed → Closed. Silence → auto-close after 3 business days with notice.
  4. If the user reopens, the same assignee picks it up — reopens are a quality signal, tracked on the dashboard.

Service Request Management

Click any step in the diagram to open its instructions below · purple pills jump to connected processes · the diagram stays pinned while you scroll.

Deliver pre-defined, pre-approved services — access, hardware, software, information — through a catalog users actually enjoy using. Requests are not incidents: nothing is broken, so the game is throughput, predictability, and automation.

Process map (BPMN)

Green circle = start · red circles = end · diamonds = decisions · purple pills = handoffs to other processes.

Goals & measures

Goals

• One unified catalog where users never have to guess whether a request goes to IT, HR, or Facilities.
• Standardize fulfillment so identical requests take identical, predictable paths.
• Automate approvals and routing — humans only touch steps that need judgment.
• Make access requests auditable end-to-end (who asked, who approved, who provisioned, when).

Procedures

2.1 — Designing a catalog item (Request Fulfillment Owner)
  1. Define the offering in user language ("I need a laptop"), not IT language ("hardware provisioning request").
  2. Build the form: only ask what fulfillment actually needs. Every extra field costs adoption.
  3. Decide the approval rule: none (pre-approved standard), line manager, budget owner, or system owner — encode it in the item's workflow, not in agents' heads.
  4. Build the fulfillment workflow in the drag-and-drop Workflow Builder: tasks, assignment groups, conditions, integrations. Test with a dry run before publishing.
  5. Attach the SLA (fulfillment target) and the related KB article ("what to expect").
  6. Publish to the right audience — InvGate Service Management supports audience-scoped catalog visibility (e.g., only managers see "new hire onboarding").
2.2 — Access requests (Fulfillment Team)
  1. Verify the approval chain completed in the ticket — never grant access on a verbal ok. The approval record is the control auditors sample.
  2. Grant exactly what was requested and approved; document the entitlement granted (system, role, permission level) in the ticket.
  3. For privileged access, add the system owner as a second approver in the item workflow.
  4. Set a review date for temporary access; an InvGate Service Management automation creates the revocation task automatically.
  5. Offboarding is the mirror image: a single "leaver" catalog item spawns revocation tasks across all systems — target completion within 1 business day of departure.
2.3 — Handling requests outside the catalog (L1 Agent)
  1. If a request arrives by email/chat for something not in the catalog, log it under a generic "Other request" item — never fulfill untracked work.
  2. If it changes production services or infrastructure, convert the work into an RFC (see Change Enablement) and link it to the request.
  3. Tag it "catalog gap". Three of the same gap in a quarter = the Request Fulfillment Owner builds a proper catalog item.

Process 3

Change Enablement

Click any step in the diagram to open its instructions below · purple pills jump to connected processes · the diagram stays pinned while you scroll.

Maximize the number of successful changes while protecting the live environment. The goal is not to slow change down — it is to make risk visible, decisions fast, and every change to production traceable to an authorization.

Process map (BPMN)

Three routes by risk: standard (pre-approved), normal (assessed, CAB-advised), emergency (act fast, document within 24 h). Purple pills = handoffs.

Goals & measures

Goals

• Every production change is assessed, authorized, scheduled, and recorded — no exceptions, including emergencies.
• Route by risk: standard changes flow without friction, normal changes get proportionate review, emergencies get speed plus retrospective rigor.
• Grow the standard-change library so the CAB reviews less, not more, over time.
• Give the service desk a change calendar it actually checks.

Procedures

3.1 — Risk assessment scoring (Change Manager)
  1. Score Likelihood of failure 1–3: proven+rehearsed / done before / novel or complex.
  2. Score Impact if it fails 1–3: single team / department or important service / org-wide or critical service.
  3. Risk = L × I. 1–2: Change Manager authorizes directly. 3–4: Change Manager + Service Owner. 6–9: CAB review required.
  4. Record scores in the RFC's custom fields so the InvGate Service Management workflow routes the approval path automatically.
  5. Auto-escalators regardless of score: touches authentication, backup, or network core; no tested backout; during a freeze period.
3.2 — Standard change library (Change Manager)
  1. Candidate criteria: performed ≥3 times as a normal change with 100% success, documented procedure, low impact, proven backout.
  2. Approve the candidate once at the CAB — that approval covers all future executions.
  3. Build it as a catalog item with its own workflow so execution is identical every time; the record is created automatically.
  4. Audit quarterly: any standard change that failed since last review returns to normal-change handling until re-proven.
  5. Target: the library grows every quarter. A shrinking CAB agenda is the health metric of this process.
3.3 — Emergency change (Change Manager / Incident Manager)
  1. Valid only to resolve an active P1/major incident or imminent security exposure. "I forgot to raise it earlier" is not an emergency.
  2. Get verbal/chat authorization from the Change Manager or designated deputy (the ECAB — two named people minimum, with an on-call rotation).
  3. Implement with a second person aware (no solo emergency changes on critical systems).
  4. Create the RFC in InvGate Service Management within 24 hours, backdated honestly, linked to the driving incident, with the approval noted.
  5. Every emergency change is reviewed at the next CAB. More than ~10% emergency rate means planning is failing — treat as a problem.
3.4 — Change calendar & freeze periods (Change Manager)
  1. Maintain the forward schedule of change in InvGate Service Management; the service desk checks it during diagnosis of every P1/P2 (procedure 1.3).
  2. Declare freeze periods (fiscal close, peak season, major business events) at least a quarter ahead; only emergency changes run during a freeze.
  3. Flag conflicts: two changes touching the same service/CI in overlapping windows require Change Manager arbitration.
  4. Publish a weekly "changes this week" announcement to the portal for business visibility.

Process 4

Problem Management

Click any step in the diagram to open its instructions below · purple pills jump to connected processes · the diagram stays pinned while you scroll.

Find and remove the root causes of incidents so they stop happening. Incident Management buys time; Problem Management buys peace. This is the process that turns a firefighting team into an engineering organization.

Process map (BPMN)

Reactive triggers (trends, major incidents) and proactive triggers feed one pipeline. Purple pills = handoffs to Knowledge and Change.

Goals & measures

Goals

• Reduce recurring incident volume by eliminating root causes.
• Publish workarounds and known errors so incidents that can't yet be prevented are at least resolved fast.
• Guarantee every major incident gets a root cause analysis — blameless, written down, acted on.
• Feed a steady stream of well-justified RFCs into Change Enablement.

Procedures

4.1 — Weekly trend review (Problem Manager)
  1. Open the incident dashboard filtered to the last 30 days: top categories by volume, top affected CIs/services, SLA breach clusters, reopen clusters.
  2. Apply the trigger rule: 3+ similar incidents in 30 days → problem candidate. "Similar" = same category + same service/CI or same symptom signature.
  3. For each candidate: create the problem record, link the source incidents (related-requests), and add a one-line hypothesis.
  4. Review the open problem backlog: anything untouched for 30 days gets a status decision — active, parked (with reason), or closed.
  5. Publish a monthly one-page summary to IT leadership: problems opened/closed, incident volume avoided, top 3 risks.
4.2 — Root cause analysis session (Problem Manager facilitates)
  1. Prepare: timeline from the ticket audit trail(s), related change records, monitoring data. Send to participants beforehand.
  2. Run blameless: the question is "what allowed this?", never "who did this?". Name systems and gaps, not people.
  3. Walk the timeline, then apply 5 Whys to the pivotal event. Stop at a cause you can act on — "human error" is never a root cause; ask what made the error easy to make and hard to catch.
  4. Distinguish: root cause vs. contributing factors vs. detection gap (why didn't we know sooner?). Each may spawn its own action.
  5. Record findings in the problem ticket: cause, evidence, actions with owners and dates. Actions become tasks or RFCs — never bullet points that die in a document.
4.3 — Known error record standard (Problem Manager + Knowledge Manager)
  1. Title states the symptom the agent will search for, not the cause ("VPN disconnects every ~60 minutes" not "DHCP lease misconfiguration").
  2. Body: symptoms → affected services/versions → workaround steps (numbered, tested) → root cause (if known) → permanent fix status with RFC link.
  3. Publish to the agent view immediately; publish a user-facing variant if the workaround is safely self-serviceable.
  4. The article and problem record link to each other; when the fix deploys, update or retire the article the same week.

Procedures

5.1 — Creating an article from a resolved ticket (any agent)
  1. From the resolved ticket flagged "KB gap", create the article draft — carry over symptoms, environment, and resolution steps.
  2. Title = what the next person will type in the search box. Test: would you find this article with the words from the original ticket?
  3. Structure: Symptoms → Environment/versions → Steps (numbered, one action each, with screenshots where a click is ambiguous) → Expected result → If this didn't work (escalation path).
  4. Strip anything user-specific (names, hostnames with real data, credentials — never credentials).
  5. Set audience (user vs. agent) and submit for review. Link the article to the source ticket.
5.2 — Monthly KB health review (Knowledge Manager)
  1. Pull the KB dashboard: articles past review date, lowest-rated articles, most-viewed articles, zero-result searches.
  2. Past-due articles: assign re-verification to the owning SME with a 2-week deadline; auto-unpublish user-facing articles 30 days past due (stale public articles destroy trust fastest).
  3. Zero-result searches: the top recurring ones become the authoring backlog — this is your users telling you exactly what to write.
  4. Low-rated articles: read the feedback comments, fix or retire within the month.
  5. Report the four KPI numbers to the Service Desk Manager; celebrate the top-contributing authors (InvGate Service Management gamification does this visibly).
5.3 — New service knowledge package (Knowledge Manager + Service Owner)
  1. No service goes live without its knowledge package: user "how do I" article, agent troubleshooting article, and the catalog item's "what to expect" text.
  2. Add this as a task in the change workflow for service-introducing changes — the RFC can't close without it.
  3. Schedule the first review at 30 days post-launch (new-service articles are always wrong somewhere).

Procedures

6.1 — Receiving, tagging & recording (IT Technician)
  1. Verify delivery against the PO (model, quantity, condition); report discrepancies to Procurement before accepting.
  2. Apply the asset tag (barcode/QR). Tag before the asset leaves the receiving area — untagged assets in circulation are how inventories rot.
  3. Create the record in InvGate Asset Management: serial number, model, PO/cost, vendor, warranty (auto-detected where supported — verify), location, status "In stock". Link the linked catalog request if one exists.
  4. Record non-connected assets (monitors, docks, lab and network gear) manually or via bulk import — discovery won't see them, but auditors will.
  5. For software: record license entitlements (quantity, metric, contract) in InvGate Asset Management at purchase, before deployment. Entitlement first, install second.
6.2 — Discovery & reconciliation (IT Asset Manager, weekly)
  1. Confirm discovery health: agent check-in rate and network discovery coverage. Chase the gap — every silent agent is a blind spot.
  2. Reconcile discovered vs. recorded: ghosts (recorded but not discovered in 30 days → locate, mark in-storage, or investigate as lost/stolen) and zombies (discovered but not recorded → tag and record, or isolate as unauthorized).
  3. Review health-status rule violations (missing encryption, outdated OS, unauthorized software) and open remediation tasks — route security-relevant findings to Security/GRC.
  4. Verify assignment accuracy for a sample of moved/returned assets against recent Service Request activity.
  5. Log the reconciliation result (accuracy %) — the trend is your inventory-accuracy KPI and your audit evidence of an operating control.
6.3 — License compliance review (IT Asset Manager, monthly)
  1. Run the license position by product: entitlements (from contracts in InvGate Asset Management) vs. installs/usage (from normalization + metering).
  2. Over-deployed: remediate before it becomes a true-up — uninstall, reharvest, or buy. Document the decision.
  3. Under-used: reclaim licenses unused >60 days (InvGate Asset Management low-usage reports); feed savings into the quarterly program report.
  4. SaaS: review usage vs. seats ahead of each renewal (procedure 6.4) — renewal negotiations without usage data are donations.
  5. Keep the position export with the month's date — a dated, repeatable license position is exactly what a software auditor asks for first.
6.4 — Contract & renewal management (IT Asset Manager + Procurement)
  1. Every contract (support, warranty, lease, SaaS, maintenance) lives in InvGate Asset Management with dates, cost, renewal terms, and the assets/licenses it covers.
  2. Alerts at 90/60/30 days before expiry route to the Asset Manager and contract owner.
  3. At 90 days: pull usage and coverage data; decide renew / renegotiate / drop with the business owner. At 30 days: escalate anything undecided to Procurement leadership.
  4. Auto-renewal clauses are flagged at intake so the 90-day review happens before the opt-out window closes, not after.
6.5 — Secure retirement & disposal (IT Technician + IT Asset Manager)
  1. Trigger: refresh calendar, failure beyond economic repair, or leaver offboarding (recovery task from the leaver catalog item — Process 2).
  2. Recover the asset and set status "Retired — pending sanitization". Remove from active management (MDM, domain, licenses reclaimed).
  3. Sanitize per NIST SP 800-88 by media and sensitivity: Clear (standard overwrite) / Purge (crypto-erase, degauss) / Destroy (shred) — ePHI and regulated data always Purge or Destroy. Record method, operator, and date on the asset.
  4. Dispose only via the certified ITAD partner (R2 or e-Stewards); obtain and attach the certificate of data destruction/recycling to the asset record.
  5. Set status "Disposed", notify Finance for book removal, and retain the record — the evidence outlives the asset.
  6. Never: donate, resell, or bin a device that hasn't completed steps 3–4. One skipped laptop is a breach notification waiting to happen.
